# Resilience as a Protocol: Measurement, Analytics, Trust Signals, and Verifiable Network Intelligence for AI Agents, IoT Systems, and Decentralized Networks

**Authors:** Johanna Moran, Manu Sheel Gupta

## Abstract

As AI agents increasingly interact with Internet of Things (IoT) environments, edge infrastructures, and decentralized systems, they are expected to make autonomous decisions based on information gathered from devices, services, peer networks, and distributed knowledge sources. Existing IETF and IRTF protocol work has significantly advanced communication, discovery, and semantic interoperability through technologies such as CoAP, CoRE Link Format, SenML, Semantic Definition Format (SDF), and related data models. These technologies enable agents and devices to discover capabilities, exchange information, and establish interoperable interactions. However, they provide limited visibility into the health, reliability, resilience, and trustworthiness of the underlying communication environment on which autonomous operation depends.

In practice, AI agents frequently operate across dynamic and heterogeneous environments where connectivity may degrade, gateways may fail, peer availability fluctuates, network partitions emerge, routing paths change, and service dependencies evolve over time. Such conditions can directly impact the correctness, safety, predictability, and trustworthiness of autonomous decisions. While network operators routinely collect measurements relating to availability, latency, topology stability, routing effectiveness, peer discovery success, and operational health, these measurements are rarely exposed as protocol-consumable information that can be interpreted by applications, devices, or autonomous agents.

This presentation proposes the concept of “Resilience as a Protocol”, where resilience is elevated from an implementation-specific operational concern to a first-class protocol capability. The central idea is that systems should be able to expose standardized resilience metadata, measurements, analytics, and trust signals that enable autonomous agents to reason about the quality and trustworthiness of their operating environment before taking action. Such signals may include connectivity confidence, peer reachability, network churn, path stability, relay dependency, discovery health, latency characteristics, topology evolution, anomaly indicators, security posture, and composite resilience scores derived from continuous measurement and observation.

The talk will draw upon operational experience from large-scale decentralized systems built using peer-to-peer technologies such as libp2p, Kademlia Distributed Hash Tables (DHTs), content-addressed networks, and edge-to-cloud architectures. Modern decentralized infrastructures already generate rich operational telemetry regarding peer discovery, routing performance, NAT traversal success, relay utilization, content availability, and network topology dynamics. These measurements offer an opportunity to create a new class of protocol-visible resilience signals that can be consumed not only by operators but also by autonomous software agents, digital twins, IoT applications, and distributed services.

A key aspect of this proposal is the exploration of standardized and verifiable representations for resilience metadata. The presentation will examine how resilience observations and trust signals can be modeled using existing IETF technologies and emerging data formats, including CBOR, deterministic CBOR profiles such as CBOR/c-42, Semantic Definition Format (SDF), and content-addressed data structures based on IPLD and Content Identifiers (CIDs). Such approaches may enable resilience information to become portable, cryptographically verifiable, composable, and shareable across organizational and administrative boundaries.

The presentation will further explore whether decentralized discovery systems such as Kademlia DHTs can serve as measurement substrates that continuously observe network conditions and generate resilience indicators. These indicators could then be represented as verifiable CBOR objects, exchanged across heterogeneous environments, and incorporated into autonomous decision-making processes. This creates a foundation for resilience-aware architectures in which agents can evaluate not only what a device or service can do, but also whether the surrounding network environment is sufficiently healthy, trustworthy, and resilient to support autonomous operation.

The talk will address several research questions relevant to the Thing-to-Thing Research Group (T2TRG): How should resilience metadata be represented, exchanged, and validated across heterogeneous systems? What resilience properties are most important for autonomous decision-making? Can resilience capabilities be described alongside device and service descriptions using SDF and related models? How should security, provenance, forensic readiness, anomaly detection, and operational trust be incorporated into resilience signaling? Can content-addressed and cryptographically verifiable representations improve trust in resilience measurements? Are new protocol abstractions required to support resilience-aware autonomous systems operating across IoT, edge, cloud, and decentralized environments?

The discussion will be grounded in practical examples involving IoT deployments, decentralized infrastructures, peer-to-peer networks, edge computing environments, and emerging AI agent ecosystems. The goal is to initiate community discussion around resilience-aware protocol architectures and identify opportunities for future IETF and IRTF research that enable autonomous systems not only to discover, communicate, and coordinate with devices and services, but also to assess and reason about the resilience and trustworthiness of the networks that connect them.
